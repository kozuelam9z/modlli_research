## Contract Paraphrase

D1 (methodology_rigor, mandatory, owned by methodology): I read this dimension as asking whether the study's design can actually support the claims it makes about human annotation as a measurement instrument. For a dataset and human-annotation paper in ML fairness, that means the annotation protocol, the scale, and the annotator and item sampling must be specified and justified. Agreement and disagreement must be quantified with statistics that suit the data type, with uncertainty reported. Any comparison between annotator groups, conditions, or scales must be designed and analysed so that confounds and chance variation are controlled. Finally, the released artefacts and procedures must be complete enough for another team to rerun the protocol and get comparable agreement estimates. This is the only dimension I score, and because it is mandatory, my rating can drive the panel outcome directly. A fatal finding leads to rejection, and a block or warn finding leads to major revision.

D2 (writing_and_structure, normal, owned by eic): I read this dimension as covering how the manuscript is organised, how clearly it explains its procedures and findings, and whether it follows the conventions expected of a method-focused venue submission, such as a findable methods section, a clear separation between protocol, results, and limitations, and the required documentation and checklists. It is outside my eligible roles, so I commit no scoring plan for it. I will mention writing problems only where unclear exposition stops me from verifying a methodological claim under D1, and the eic role keeps sole ownership of the D2 rating.

## Scoring Plan

### D1: methodology_rigor
dimension_id: D1
what_to_look_for: Whether the paper (a) states the annotation construct and the scale used, with justification and full annotator instructions, including how out-of-scope or ambiguous items are handled; (b) gives the size, recruitment channel, demographics, compensation, training, and screening of the annotator pool, and describes item sampling, including the distribution of image conditions such as lighting, resolution, pose, and occlusion, plus whether the annotator-by-item design is fully crossed, nested, or sparse; (c) uses agreement statistics that suit ordinal or interval labels and the overlap design, such as Krippendorff's alpha with an ordinal or interval metric or an ICC of the appropriate form, rather than only raw percent agreement or nominal kappa, with confidence intervals from a bootstrap or resampling scheme that respects both the annotator and item dimensions; (d) separates true subjectivity from noise, for example with within-annotator test-retest reliability, attention or gold checks, or modelling of annotator and item effects; (e) tests group or condition differences with appropriate models, such as mixed effects with annotator and item random effects, and reports effect sizes and corrections for multiple comparisons; (f) states and examines the sensitivity of any consensus or aggregation rule (majority vote, median, mean, or a probabilistic label model), including its effect on downstream fairness metrics or subgroup assignments; (g) justifies any external reference treated as ground truth, such as colorimetric measurements; and (h) releases or documents annotator-level raw labels, protocol materials, analysis code, and ethics and consent information, with a datasheet or equivalent.
what_triggers_block: A central agreement, subjectivity, or group-difference claim rests on an analysis that is wrong but fixable. Examples are a nominal statistic applied to ordinal labels, CIs that ignore the annotator or item clustering, group differences confounded with item assignment or presentation order, aggregation rules applied without any sensitivity analysis even though the conclusions depend on them, or annotator demographics and sampling that are undocumented while the paper still makes claims about the effect of annotator identity. Any of these can be repaired with reanalysis, added controls, or new documentation without collecting new data.
what_triggers_warn: The core claims are plausibly supported, but there are reporting or robustness gaps that a careful reader would need closed. Examples are missing confidence intervals or effect sizes on secondary results, no correction for multiple comparisons in exploratory subgroup tests, partial annotator instructions or recruitment details, raw annotator-level labels or code released only on request, untested sensitivity to the choice of scale or to image conditions, or limitations that understate threats to generalising beyond the sampled pool or image distribution.
what_triggers_fatal: The design cannot answer the stated research question even with reanalysis, so new data collection or a redesigned study would be needed. Examples are subjectivity or consensus claims made without any repeated or overlapping annotations from which agreement could be estimated, agreement computed only on pre-aggregated labels, comparisons between annotator groups where group membership is perfectly confounded with item sets or platforms, headline conclusions contradicted by the paper's own reported numbers, or human-subjects annotation of identifiable faces with no stated ethics review or consent basis where the venue requires one.
